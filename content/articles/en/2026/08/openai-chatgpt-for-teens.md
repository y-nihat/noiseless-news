## What happened

OpenAI began rolling out "ChatGPT for Teens" on 18 August 2026, a version of
ChatGPT built for 13-to-17-year-olds [1][5][9]. Anyone the system estimates
to be under 18, or who states their age falls between 13 and 17, is
automatically placed into it. OpenAI says it does not verify identity
directly; instead it estimates age from a mix of self-reported age and
behavioral and account signals — a probabilistic "age prediction" system,
not ID-based verification, with an appeals process for people it gets wrong
[1][3][6]. OpenAI said the global rollout would take about two weeks to
complete, with some markets, including Australia, following in early
September [5][9].

The teen version blocks romantic or sexual chat, and restricts conversations
around self-harm, eating disorders and violence; it also stops the model
from suggesting it has personal feelings for the user or that it is
conscious [1][4][6]. It adds teen-specific onboarding and learning-focused
starter prompts intended to help with studying rather than simply supplying
answers [1].

Parents and guardians can optionally link a teen's account to a parental
controls dashboard. From there they can set "Quiet Hours" to limit access
during scheduled times and turn on "Study Hours" so new chats default into a
study mode. They are notified only if automated systems and a human reviewer
flag a possible safety risk in a conversation, and that notification
summarizes the concern rather than quoting the teen's words. OpenAI is
explicit that parental controls do not otherwise let a parent or guardian
read or monitor a teen's conversations [1][2][4].

## Why it matters

The rollout follows lawsuits alleging AI chatbots have harmed minors,
including a wrongful-death suit against OpenAI that it disputes, and arrives
as California's SB 243 — in effect since 1 January 2026 — imposes new rules
on AI companion chatbots used by minors [8][4]. TechCrunch, covering the
launch, noted the safeguards arrive years after ChatGPT already had a large
teenage user base, and questioned how well the controls will hold up against
teens determined to work around them; child-safety advocates quoted in
coverage of the launch voiced similar doubts about whether the changes go
far enough [7][4]. The design leaves the underlying tension unresolved
rather than eliminated: age placement still rests on prediction and
self-report instead of verified identity, and linked parents get schedule
controls and risk alerts, not access to what a teen actually writes.
