## What happened

Taiwan's Keelung District Prosecutors' Office has indicted eight people,
including a manager at Nvidia's Taiwan office prosecutors describe as the
scheme's alleged central figure, over a plan to illegally export AI servers
to China [1][2][3]. A ninth suspect, the head of a separate firm allegedly
involved, remains at large and has not been indicted [1][2][3].

Prosecutors allege 74 Nvidia B300 (Blackwell-family) AI servers were
illegally exported to China — 50 routed through Indonesia, 16 sent
directly, and 8 via Japan then Hong Kong — while a further 56 servers were
seized before leaving Taiwan [2][1]. They are seeking a maximum five-year
prison sentence for the Nvidia manager and three other defendants on
export-control and document-forgery charges [2][4][6]. Some defendants
additionally face a separate, more serious breach-of-trust charge tied to
roughly NT$39 million (about $1.2-1.3 million) in company assets they
allegedly diverted; prosecutors put the scheme's total illicit proceeds at
more than $21 million [1].

Neither Nvidia nor Supermicro has been accused of wrongdoing as a company;
prosecutors reportedly described both companies' internal export-control
procedures as rigorous, and each company said it is cooperating with
authorities or improving its compliance process [2][5]. The Nvidia manager
at the center of the case was reportedly first detained by Taiwanese
authorities in late July 2026, before this week's indictment [8]. In May
2026, after Taiwan's earlier detention of Supermicro employees in an
earlier phase of the same investigation, Nvidia chief executive Jensen
Huang said he hoped those involved would "enhance and improve their
regulation compliance" [8][7].

This Taiwan case is unrelated to a separate criminal case filed in the
United States in March 2026 against different individuals connected to
Supermicro's Taiwan office, over a much larger alleged export scheme; the
two cases share no defendants [8].

## Why it matters

No court has ruled on any of these allegations. None of the defendants is
named here: prosecutors have released only roles, not full identities, and
the case does not depend on a name to be understood — that also avoids any
confusion with the separate, unrelated US case. The prosecution is a rare
criminal case built around alleged export-control evasion involving
Nvidia's newest AI server hardware, arriving as Washington's chip-export
restrictions to China remain a central flashpoint in AI policy.
