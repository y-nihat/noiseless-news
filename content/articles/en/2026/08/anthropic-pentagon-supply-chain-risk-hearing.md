## What happened

The Pentagon designated Anthropic a national-security "supply chain risk" in March 2026, after the company refused to lift restrictions barring its Claude models from use in fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance of Americans — red lines Anthropic had set during stalled contract talks [2][3]. Days later, Judge Rita Lin of the US District Court for the Northern District of California granted Anthropic a preliminary injunction blocking enforcement of the designation. Anthropic separately appealed a related order to the DC Circuit; that appeals panel declined in April to pause the designation while the case proceeds, and heard oral argument in May, with judges on the panel appearing divided on the government's authority to make the call [3].

On 30 July, Lin held a hearing on cross-motions for summary judgment — each side asking her to rule in its favor without a trial. She opened by telling the government that, "if anything," the record "has gotten worse" for its position, and said she saw no evidence supporting the Pentagon's argument that Anthropic could "alter the model after it was delivered or flip some kind of kill switch" [1][2]. She also pressed government lawyers on whether the Pentagon's stated rationale — that Anthropic's public criticism of its contracting demands justified branding the company untrustworthy — reflected its actual position, calling that idea "extreme" [2].

Lin took both motions under submission and will issue a written ruling; neither side got a decision from the bench, and no date has been set [1][2].

## Why it matters

This is the first US government use of a "supply chain risk" designation — a label previously reserved for foreign adversaries — against a domestic AI lab, and it turns on whether a company can be penalized for refusing specific military and surveillance use cases while under contract talks. A ruling for Anthropic would reinforce that contractors keep First Amendment protection when they push back on government demands; a ruling for the Pentagon would hand agencies a broader tool to pressure AI vendors on model-use terms. No court has yet ruled on the merits at trial.
