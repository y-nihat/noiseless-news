## What happened

A working paper from Stanford's Digital Economy Lab — "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence," by economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen — finds that employment among workers aged 22-25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is now about 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with employment among similarly aged workers in less-exposed occupations, per the paper's August 12, 2026 update [1]. That gap has widened since the study first published in August 2025, when it stood at roughly 13% [1][2]. Experienced workers in the same occupations show no comparable gap, and the decline traces mainly to reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased departures of those already employed [1].

The paper, based on ADP payroll data covering millions of U.S. workers, is a working paper — not yet peer-reviewed [1][2]. Its authors are explicit about the limits of what they've shown: they say they find "no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement" from AI, and describe their own findings as "early, descriptive indicators... rather than causal estimates" [1]. The "young worker" category is defined by age (22-25) within an occupation, not by job title or seniority [1].

Not every economist attributes the pattern to AI. Separate research from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found that remote-work trends, not AI, account for the larger share of the broader rise in unemployment among recent college graduates — a live, unresolved dispute among economists over how much of the gap AI actually explains [3].

## Why it matters

If young workers are consistently the first to lose ground as AI capability grows, that has direct consequences for hiring pipelines and early-career prospects across AI-exposed fields — but the study's own authors caution against reading their descriptive pattern as proof of a single cause, and independent research disagrees on how much of it is AI versus other forces like remote work and the broader hiring slowdown.
