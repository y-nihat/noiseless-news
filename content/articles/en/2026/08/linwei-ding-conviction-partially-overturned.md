## What happened

U.S. District Judge Vince Chhabria, of the Northern District of California,
on 20 August 2026 vacated seven economic-espionage counts against former
Google software engineer Linwei Ding [1][3][4]. The judge ruled that
prosecutors had not proven Ding intended or knew his conduct would benefit
the Chinese government — a required element of the economic-espionage
charge [1][3]. Seven separate counts of theft of trade secrets from the
same trial were not disturbed; Chhabria found the evidence supported those
convictions [1][3].

Ding, a Chinese national who joined Google in 2019, was found guilty on all
14 original counts in a jury verdict at the end of January 2026, after an
11-day trial [2]. Prosecutors alleged he uploaded thousands of pages of
confidential information about Google's AI-supercomputing hardware and
software to a personal cloud account between 2022 and 2023, while secretly
affiliated with two China-based technology companies — at one he was in
talks to become chief technology officer, and he had founded and led the
other as chief executive [2]. Neither company has been named in reporting
on the case.

Ding remains convicted on the seven trade-secret-theft counts and is
scheduled to be sentenced on those on 1 September 2026 [1][3].

## Why it matters

The ruling narrows, rather than overturns, a marquee US prosecution built
around alleged AI-related corporate espionage — one of the first major
cases centered on stolen AI-supercomputing infrastructure design rather
than model weights or code. Ding still faces sentencing on felony
trade-secret charges; the case is a live test of how US courts apply the
government-benefit element that separates economic espionage from ordinary
trade-secret theft.
