## What happened

Google won a bankruptcy auction for a batch of internal business data from
Spirit Airlines, which shut down in May 2026, agreeing to pay $10 million
and beating a $7.5 million backup bid from AI hiring platform Mercor
[1][2]. The data includes roughly 100 million internal emails and 500
million Microsoft Teams chats, along with source code, financial,
operations and competitor-pricing records [1][2]. Customer records and
Free Spirit loyalty-program data — covering tens of millions of
passengers — are explicitly excluded from the sale, and the remaining
records will be stripped of personally identifiable information before
transfer, according to a court filing by Dylan Friesner of PJT Partners,
the investment bank handling Spirit's estate [2].

Google confirmed the deal, saying the data "can be helpful in improving
our products and AI models" [1]. The sale is not yet final: a federal
bankruptcy judge, Sean H. Lane, must approve it in the U.S. Bankruptcy
Court for the Southern District of New York, case no. 1:25-bk-11897
[1][2][3]. The hearing originally scheduled for 19 August 2026 to
consider the data sale specifically was adjourned before it took place,
according to the case docket; a new hearing is now set for 9 September
2026 [3]. The Association of Flight Attendants-CWA, which represents
Spirit's flight attendants, has since formally filed an objection in the
court docket [1][3], after telling Forbes it would do so and calling the
sale "outrageous" [4]. The union's filing seeks a review protocol for
labor and disciplinary records and a bar on using the data to profile
identifiable flight attendants, rather than an outright block on the
sale [1][3].

## Why it matters

Most AI data-sourcing disputes to date have centered on scraped public web
content. This deal is different: it is Google acquiring a defunct
company's internal corporate records — communications and operational
data never meant for public release — through a bankruptcy estate sale, a
route with few established norms for what buyers may do with the
material. The deliberate exclusion of customer and loyalty data, and the
PII-scrubbing step before transfer, show the deal was structured with
privacy objections in mind. The adjournment of the sale hearing and a
union objection show that structuring has not settled the question;
whether the court approves the sale, and on what terms, remains open.
