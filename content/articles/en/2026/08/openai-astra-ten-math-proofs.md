## What happened

On 1 August 2026, OpenAI published a post claiming that an internal,
unreleased model it calls Astra produced Lean-certified proofs or
constructions addressing ten long-standing open problems in mathematics and
theoretical computer science, including a new bound on high-dimensional
sphere packing and a construction of a non-sofic group [1]. OpenAI's own
post disclosed that people helped prepare the manuscripts and formalize the
proofs, and said the company takes responsibility for their correctness [1].

Two mathematicians have since raised attribution concerns about specific
results. Steven Miller of Yeshiva University says the sphere-packing proof
reuses an argument that first appeared in a 2016 paper he co-authored,
without proper credit: "They are running roughshod over the work of others
who came before them in a deliberate way," he said, calling the pattern
"research misconduct" [2]. Separately, Francesco Fournier-Facio of Cambridge
says a different result — a construction related to non-sofic groups —
combines ideas already present in a 2016 and a 2019 paper. His complaint is
narrower than Miller's and does not allege misconduct: he says OpenAI's
mathematicians "did their best to attribute these ideas correctly" within
the paper itself, and directs his criticism instead at the announcement's
publicity framing, which he says overstates the result's novelty —
OpenAI's "big PR machine that wants to sound as impressive as possible and
does not care about being 100 percent accurate" [2].

An OpenAI spokesperson responded that the company "take[s] responsibility
for the correctness of these results and are meeting the same standards
generally expected of human mathematicians," and said OpenAI plans "small
updates" to the paper "this week, consistent with standard academic
practice" [2]. The response did not dispute Miller's or Fournier-Facio's
underlying citation claims.

## Why it matters

OpenAI has made a similar overclaim before: in October 2025, an OpenAI vice
president posted and then deleted a claim that the company's AI had solved
ten Erdős problems, which mathematician Thomas Bloom showed was a
literature-search artifact rather than new mathematics. That the same lab
draws a second, more serious attribution dispute on its next major
math-claims push — this time framed by a named academic as deliberate,
systematic misconduct rather than a one-off overclaim — is a meaningful
test of whether AI labs publishing mathematics results are holding
themselves to normal academic citation standards before, not after,
publicity. OpenAI's acknowledgment and promised correction suggest the labs
themselves may not yet have a citation-review step ahead of these
announcements.
