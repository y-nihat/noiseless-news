## What happened

Sony Music Entertainment and nine affiliated labels, including Arista
Records and LaFace, filed a new copyright-infringement lawsuit against AI
music generator Udio in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District
of New York on 20 July 2026, asserting 30,117 sound recordings [1][2]. The
filing follows a 29 June 2026 ruling in which the same court denied Sony's
motion to add these recordings to its existing 2024 case against Udio —
the judge acknowledged Sony's right to separately pursue claims over them,
which Sony cites as grounds for the new complaint [1][2].

The complaint seeks statutory damages of up to $150,000 per infringed
work — a theoretical maximum above $4.5 billion across all 30,117 works —
plus up to $2,500 for each Digital Millennium Copyright Act circumvention
violation, and an injunction [1][2]. Sony's complaint states: "Udio's
belated embrace of licensing only underscores the unlawfulness of its
decision to copy [the labels'] copyrighted sound recordings, without a
license, in the first place" [1].

Universal Music Group and Warner Music Group, Sony's co-plaintiffs in the
original 2024 case against Udio, have each since reached licensing
agreements with the company, leaving Sony as the only major label still
litigating against Udio [2]. Udio did not comment on the new suit. In the
original case, it argued fair use, saying its models learn from examples
the way a student studies music, and separately acknowledged training on a
large collection of recordings that presumably included ones owned by the
labels [1][2]. No court has ruled on the allegations in the new complaint.

## Why it matters

The filing shows the major-label AI-training litigation splitting apart:
two of the three majors have converted their claims into licensing
revenue, while Sony is betting a jury will value its catalog higher than a
negotiated deal — with statutory damages exposure now running into the
billions if it wins.
