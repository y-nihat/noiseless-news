## What happened

The European Commission's "AI Omnibus" — a package of amendments to the EU AI Act, formally the Digital Omnibus on AI — entered into force on 27 July 2026, the Commission said [1]. The Commission describes it as a "targeted simplification of the AI rulebook while preserving strong safeguards for people's safety and fundamental rights" [1]; it was first proposed in November 2025 as part of a broader digital-omnibus package.

The package's main effect is timing: compliance for most high-risk AI systems under the AI Act's Annex III — including systems used in employment and education — now begins 2 December 2027, more than a year later than the original August 2026 deadline. High-risk AI embedded in physical products such as machinery, toys and lifts gets until 2 August 2028 [1]. The Commission also says the package extends small-and-medium-enterprise compliance protections to small mid-cap companies, expands regulatory sandboxes including an EU-level sandbox, and simplifies AI-literacy and database-registration requirements [1].

Two provisions move the other direction: the package bans AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery or child sexual abuse material, and it hands the EU AI Office expanded oversight of general-purpose AI models and of AI systems deployed on major platforms [1].

The changes were contested before they took effect. European Digital Rights (EDRi), a coalition of digital-rights groups, had urged EU lawmakers to reject the package, calling it "a rollback of AI safeguards" that would leave regulators, researchers and affected people with less information to check whether AI providers are classifying their systems correctly [2].

## Why it matters

This is the first substantive rewrite of the AI Act's timeline since the law took effect, and it lands before the original high-risk compliance deadline it replaces — companies building AI for employment, education and other Annex III use cases now have over a year longer before the strictest obligations apply. Whether that counts as a pragmatic fix for implementation delays (the Commission's framing) or a weakening of protections before they ever took effect (EDRi's framing) is a live dispute that will likely resurface as the new December 2027 deadline approaches.
