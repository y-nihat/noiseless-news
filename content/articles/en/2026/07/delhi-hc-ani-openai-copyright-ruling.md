## What happened

On 24 July 2026, Justice Amit Bansal of the Delhi High Court dismissed an application by Indian news agency ANI for an interim injunction that would have barred OpenAI from using ANI's published content to train ChatGPT's underlying models [1][2][3][4][5][6]. The case is ANI Media Pvt Ltd v. Open AI OpCo LLC (2026:DHC:5900).

The court held that OpenAI's storage of ANI's articles for model training prima facie falls within Section 52(1)(a)(i) of India's Copyright Act, 1957 — an exception covering "private or personal use, including research" — and can constitute fair dealing [1][2][3][4]. Justice Bansal also reasoned that requiring AI developers to obtain licences from every copyright holder before training would make model development commercially impractical [1][6]. Separately, the court found ANI had not shown that ChatGPT's RAG-generated responses were substantially similar to, reproduced, or memorised its original articles [1][2][3][4].

Procedurally, this order decides only ANI's request for interim relief. ANI's underlying copyright suit against OpenAI continues before the same court, and the judgment expressly states that its prima facie findings set no precedent and do not determine the case's eventual outcome [1][3][4][6]. Two questions are explicitly left open for trial: whether ChatGPT's RAG outputs amount to "communication to the public," and whether an AI model's retained training data itself constitutes reproduction under copyright law [3].

ANI first sued OpenAI in November 2024, alleging both unauthorised training use and that ChatGPT had attributed fabricated stories to the agency; the attribution allegation is separate from this training-copyright ruling and is not resolved by it [4][5].

## Why it matters

Indian legal and business press are describing this as the country's first substantive court ruling on whether training an AI model on copyrighted news content infringes copyright [1][2][3][5], giving OpenAI and other AI developers an early, though non-binding, read on how an Indian court views the fair-dealing exception for AI training. Because it is an interim order on a preliminary application, it does not settle the law: ANI's suit continues, the reproduction and "communication to the public" questions remain to be tried, and either side could seek to challenge this order in the meantime. No public statement from ANI on next steps, including any appeal, had surfaced as of publication.
